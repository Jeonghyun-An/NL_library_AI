The Effects of Lead Time Variability on Managing Component Replenishment for Customized Manufacturing

• Impact of M on the optimal performance as a function of c_h^1: In <Table 4>, 15 examples are grouped into 3 sets by c_h^1 = 3, 5, and 7. We observe that given M(>1), an increase in c_h^1 enlarges the % value. We also observed that given M, Q* is not nearly affected by the increase in c_h^1. This observation implies that as service delay becomes more expensive, reducing lead time variability becomes more effective than increasing the replenishment order quantity.

• Impact of M on the optimal performance as a function of c_R: <Table 5> presents 15 examples grouped into 3 sets by c_R = 25, 50, and 75. Given M(>1), the % value increases as the customer order rejection cost increases. In addition, given M(>1), Q* is not nearly affected by the increase in c_R. Analogous to the case of c_h^1, this test implies that if order rejection becomes expensive, reducing lead time variability is more effective than increasing the replenishment order quantity.

• Impact of M on the optimal performance as a function of c_K: In <Table 6>, we group 15 examples into 3 sets by c_K = 100, 200, and 300. <Table 6> shows that given M(>1), an increase in c_K tends to shrink the % value. Since the increase in c_K restricts the inventory replenishment, reducing lead time variability becomes more effective than increasing the replenishment order quantity.
